Gems of wisdom.

Keep the test that works.

Save the LED outline, audio loop and LCD bench target before adding another subsystem. A small repeatable test helps separate wiring, driver and gameplay failures. Label simulated inputs clearly.

Make the hardware observable.

Print distance, echo duration, validity and state changes over UART. Use a known flat target to check ranging. If a USB serial adapter is absent from the operating system, fix the connection before rewriting working firmware.

Change one layer at a time.

Prove the amplifier and speaker with a repeating sound before connecting the sound to the sensor. Prove fixed graphics before animation. Then follow the data from input to decision to output.

Share one pin and timer plan.

Our older single-controller design and separate audio prototype reused some pins for different jobs. Splitting the work between two PIC32s made controller ownership part of the interface too. Keep schematic, firmware and wiring synchronized.

Account for waiting.

An interrupt can keep audio samples flowing while foreground code waits for an echo, but that wait still delays other foreground jobs. Use timeouts, bounded interrupt work and event-driven gameplay timing.

Verify the interface, not just the part number.

The LCD’s red/blue reversal was a color-order setting. Amplifier input pads and speaker output pads have different jobs. Start with a simple pattern or tone before building a complicated interface.

Measure the snack before making the chute.

Our dispenser did not fit a full Gushers pouch, so individual pieces were used. Test the actual reward and packaging before committing to the mechanism. Check dispensing reliability and final food handling.

Document the machine you actually built.

A buildable scaffold is not proof of completed gameplay. Push the demonstrated code, preserve attribution, photograph wiring, record dimensions and save receipts. Separate actual results from design intent and lab-supplied parts from personally purchased materials.
