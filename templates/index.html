THE ONE-PAGE FUNCTION STORY

One tiny kitchen.
Three ways to play.

Add. Heat. Stir. Try to keep the dish together while the machine brings your cooking to life.

Read the stand-alone function page

Panic at the Pan is a tabletop cooking arcade. A laser-cut enclosure holds a 3D-printed pan and spatula, five ingredient switches, a joystick, a distance sensor, displays, speaker and reward mechanism.

The green Start and red Stop buttons light only when their actions are available. Two chained LED matrix displays tell the player the dish being cooked, time and score. Turning an ingredient switch on displays an ingredient icon on the e-paper screen in the middle of the pan.

A hand hovering over the ultrasonic sensor sets the simulated heat: closer is hotter, farther is cooler. The LCD shows a varying heat bar, while the speaker plays a simple song and distance-dependent sizzle. The joystick controls the back-and-forth sweep of a small servo-driven spatula.

Two PIC32MX170F256B controllers share the work. One handles the LCD, sensor and audio. The other runs the main game logic and remaining functions. The reward is individual Gushers pieces—the dispenser turned out too small for a full pouch.

The team reports that the complete game worked at grading and received full credit on all graded items. Exact recipes, scoring and final timing await the grading code’s Git push. The original checkpoint targeted a roughly 60-second round, a 30-second minimum and a 20-second inactivity timeout; those are design targets until verified against that code.

PERIPHERAL PIC32

Gives the heat a voice.

  • Trigger the ultrasonic sensor and time its echo
  • Convert distance into heat feedback
  • Update the ST7789 LCD heat bar
  • Synthesize music, frying texture and chimes
  • Drive PWM audio into the filter and amplifier
TX ↔ RX
They talk. They don’t share a clock wire.

Checkpoint 2 proposes a 3.3 V UART2 link, 115200 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit. TX pin 21 connects to the other PIC32’s RX pin 17 in each direction, with common ground. The final protocol and settings await the flashed code.

GEMS OF WISDOM

Things we’d tell
our past selves.

A few well-earned notes for the next generation of 218ers. Open the stand-alone wisdom page.

✳

Measure the snack.
Then build the dispenser.

Our dispenser didn’t fit a complete Gushers pouch. The final game used individual pieces. Test the exact reward and packaging before committing to the chute.

THE MOST LITERAL BOTTLENECK.

Save the test that works.

Keep the LED outline, audio loop and LCD bench targets. A known good test tells you which layer broke when the full system goes quiet.

Make invisible things visible.

Print distance, echo duration and state changes over UART. Debug a sensor with a flat target at a known distance before relying on a waving hand.

Two boards still need one plan.

Pin maps, timer assignments and controller responsibilities are shared interfaces. Update the code, schematic and wiring together.

Timing is part of the design.

Audio interrupts keep samples moving, but an echo wait can still block foreground work. Use timeouts and bound the time spent in each job.

Keep the evidence as you go.

Photograph wiring. Save receipts. Record dimensions. Push the actual demonstrated code. The report should describe the machine you built.
