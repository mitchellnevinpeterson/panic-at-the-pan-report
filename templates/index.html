THE ONE-PAGE FUNCTION STORY

One tiny kitchen.
Three ways to play.

Add. Heat. Stir. Try to keep the dish together while the machine brings your cooking to life.

Read the stand-alone function page

Panic at the Pan is a tabletop cooking arcade. A laser-cut enclosure holds a 3D-printed pan and spatula, five ingredient switches, a joystick, a distance sensor, displays, speaker and reward mechanism.

The green Start and red Stop buttons light only when their actions are available. Two chained LED matrix displays tell the player the dish being cooked, time and score. Turning an ingredient switch on displays an ingredient icon on the e-paper screen in the middle of the pan.

A hand hovering over the ultrasonic sensor sets the simulated heat: closer is hotter, farther is cooler. The LCD shows a varying heat bar, while the speaker plays a simple song and distance-dependent sizzle. The joystick controls the back-and-forth sweep of a small servo-driven spatula.

Two PIC32MX170F256B controllers share the work. One handles the LCD, sensor and audio. The other runs the main game logic and remaining functions. The reward is individual Gushers pieces—the dispenser turned out too small for a full pouch.

The team reports that the complete game worked at grading and received full credit on all graded items. The integrated source now documents a 60-second cooking round, Finish enabled after 30 seconds, and a 20-second inactivity return. The repository identifies checked-off (b877530) as hardware verified. The latest wire_management branch (0ffeea5) contains later changes that require separate hardware confirmation.

PIC32MX170F256B

MAIN GAME PIC32

Keeps the kitchen running.

  • Main game state machine and game logic
  • Receive switch/button events; read the local joystick
  • E-paper ingredient icons in the pan
  • Two chained LED matrix display assemblies
  • Servo-related functions and reward control

PERIPHERAL PIC32

Gives the heat a voice.

  • Trigger the ultrasonic sensor and time its echo
  • Read MCP23S17 buttons/switches and drive lamps
  • Update the ST7789 LCD heat bar
  • Synthesize music, frying texture and burn feedback
  • Drive PWM audio into the filter and amplifier
TX ↔ RX
They talk. They don’t share a clock wire.

The integrated UART2 adapter uses crossed TX pin 21 / RX pin 17, common ground and nominal 115200 baud, 8N1 (BRG=42 gives about 116279 baud at 20 MHz). Escaped 23-byte frames carry CRC16, epoch and sequence fields. PIC1 sends 100 ms heartbeats with LCD/audio/lamp commands; PIC2 returns range data and acknowledged input events. Retries and deduplication prevent a lost acknowledgment from adding an ingredient twice.

INTEGRATED GAMEBUTTONS BUILD

Run the round.

GameSM owns Welcome, dish introduction, 60-second cooking, Finish eligibility after 30 seconds, and the result/reset sequence. Twenty seconds without activity returns to Welcome without a treat.

Welcome·Cooking·Result / timeout
The recipe, decoded.

The five switches are Protein, Carb, Vegetables, Spice and Oil. Their heat targets are 14, 24, 32, 28 and 10 cm, with a ±4 cm JUST RIGHT band. Correct ingredients earn +10; wrong ones cost 10. Heat earns up to +1/s; three seconds TOO MUCH burns the current ingredient for −10. The score stays at or above zero.

Checked-off serves chicken fried rice: Protein, Carb, Spice and Oil are correct. The latest branch adds chicken/broccoli stir-fry (all except Carb) and pasta aglio e olio (Carb, Spice and Oil), selected without repeating the previous dish.

Checked-off scores stirring time and gives +2 for each of the first two flips per ingredient. The latest branch instead scores full joystick circles (+1 up to targets 3/5/0/6/0, then −1) and ingredient-specific flips (+2 up to 2/4/1/3/0, then −2). A score of at least 50 earns the Gushers.

12 µs

Send the ping.

A trigger pulse starts ultrasonic ranging. The core timer timestamps ECHO edges; round-trip time becomes distance.

100 ms

Take another look.

The non-blocking ranger waits 100 ms after each result before another attempt. Each echo edge wait has a 30 ms timeout.

80 µs

Make the next sample.

A 12.5 kHz interrupt mixes music, sizzle and a burn cue. The latest branch suppresses zone chimes; checked-off retains them.

GEMS OF WISDOM

Things we’d tell
our past selves.

A few well-earned notes for the next generation of 218ers. Open the stand-alone wisdom page.

✳

Measure the snack.
Then build the dispenser.

Our dispenser didn’t fit a complete Gushers pouch. The final game used individual pieces. Test the exact reward and packaging before committing to the chute.

THE MOST LITERAL BOTTLENECK.

Save the test that works.

Keep the LED outline, audio loop and LCD bench targets. A known good test tells you which layer broke when the full system goes quiet.

Make invisible things visible.

Print distance, echo duration and state changes over UART. Debug a sensor with a flat target at a known distance before relying on a waving hand.

Two boards still need one plan.

Pin maps, timer assignments and controller responsibilities are shared interfaces. Update the code, schematic and wiring together.

Timing is part of the design.

The integrated ranger and e-paper driver use non-blocking states. Short foreground jobs keep game events and communication moving while interrupts maintain audio.

Keep the evidence as you go.

Photograph wiring. Save receipts. Record dimensions. Push the actual demonstrated code. The report should describe the machine you built.
